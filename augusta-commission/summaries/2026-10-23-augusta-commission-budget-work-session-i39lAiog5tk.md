### Augusta Utilities: system overview and 2027 budget (Director Wes Byne)

* System: five water plants (three groundwater, two surface water), two wastewater plants, about 1,300 miles of water main, and about 1,000 miles of sewer main [00:08:56]. The utility produces about 50 MGD against a permitted 92 MGD. Wastewater averages about 32 MGD against a rated 46 MGD [00:09:21].
* Staffing: about 300 of 350 positions filled, plus about 25 temps on any day [00:09:51].
* Personnel costs in 2026 were about $18 million direct and $7 million indirect, about $26 million total. The 2027 figure dips slightly because of key vacancies [00:10:24].
* The proposed 2027 operating budget rises about $2.6 million [00:10:45]. Supplies rise about $6 million, mostly meter inventory [00:10:54]. Capital asks for an extra $5 million for building renovations and water and sewer pipe [00:11:05]. Interfund transfers run about $30 million a year [00:11:15].
* Expenses run about $105 million without capital and $126 million with capital, against about $133 million in revenue. The transcript says "2017," which appears to be a mistranscription of 2027 [00:11:26]. Debt service is about $37 million [00:11:43].
* The final position is about $2.5 million without capital and about $19 million negative with capital, which Byne attributed to bond financing. If the $12.7 million and $6 million revenue-financed capital accounts are not spent, the budget is "basically back to even" [00:11:47].

### Utilities capital projects and funding sources

* Canal embankment repair, Phase 1 (pumping station to I-20): about $7 million. GEFA's Helene SRF offers 50% principal forgiveness and 0% interest on the rest, and its board takes it up in February [00:12:41].
* Bulkhead gates (the Lake Olmstead flood control structure for downtown): $6 million [00:13:32].
* Travis Boykin Road water and sewer: $3.5 million from a 2021 congressionally directed spending earmark (Senator Warnock). It goes to bid in the next two months [00:13:41] [00:25:35].
* Woodlake sewer: a $2 million OneGeorgia/DCA grant, now in environmental review [00:14:04].
* South Augusta Sewer Extension into the Little Spirit Creek Basin: about $10 million from SPLOST 9 [00:14:29].
* Highland Avenue filter upgrades: now $7 million, up from a $2 million budget four years ago for two filters. The building handles about 40% of treatment capacity [00:14:46].
* Old filter building roof and structural work: about $5.5 million, likely funded with a GEFA SRF loan [00:15:30].
* J.B. Messerly: $18 million already approved. Work has started on $4.5 million of polishing pond repairs and $5.5 million for digesters 4 and 5 [00:15:57].
* AMI meter replacement: about 4,000 cellular meters are installed so far. Byne wants to finish in three years instead of five, funded by an approved 20-year GEFA SRF loan [00:16:17].
* Inflow and infiltration reduction is tied to the consent order [00:16:45].
* Other funding pursued [00:17:22]:
  * About $59 million in Helene SRF grant and loan opportunities, with approvals expected in February [00:17:37].
  * A $3.5 million grant for PFAS treatment study [00:17:55].
  * About $25 million in SRF loans for the Camp Hancock and Old Wrightsboro Road areas [00:18:15].
  * A Corps of Engineers water infrastructure loan program capped at $50 million, for canal work such as the diversion dam [00:18:52].
  * A $20 million DCIP grant application in June for Fort Gordon groundwater infrastructure [00:19:35].
* FEMA: the city requested $36 million (Byne estimates about $40 million) for canal embankment damage. The city is in arbitration after a denied first appeal. Final briefings are due in 45 days, and a decision is unlikely before March or April 2027. Byne could not guarantee any award [00:19:47].
* Most grants need about a 20% local match, which is part of why capital funds are held in reserve [00:26:48].
* An unidentified commissioner asked whether the projects had been procured. Byne said the figures are engineers' estimates [00:25:12]. If FEMA reimburses the embankment, the Helene SRF money would be reassigned to other projects such as Messerly or Highland Avenue [00:26:04].

### Regulatory issues and cost inflation

* Drought: lakes are down 10 to 11 feet, and Hartwell has a structural issue. The Corps of Engineers, not the city, controls river flow [00:27:21]. State-driven watering restrictions are possible in the spring [00:28:19].
* Lead and copper rule improvements: the city must expose connectors at 70,000 water connections by November 2027 and is waiting on EPA guidance. Routine testing has shown no issue [00:28:29].
* PFAS: studies show elevated levels in Georgia rivers and in rainfall. Byne called PFAS and lead the two biggest regulatory cost burdens for the next 10 years [00:29:30]. The consent order is progressing [00:30:21].
* Three-year cost trends: pipe roughly doubled and asphalt rose about 40%, partly because utilities now pays for wider road overlays with Engineering [00:30:47]. Chemicals rose about 15% [00:31:36]. Running the canal diesels costs about $0.5 million a month at $2.50 a gallon, or about $1 million a month at $6 a gallon [00:32:12].

### Utilities rate and fee recommendation

* Byne requested a 6.5% rate increase for 2027. The 2024 bond requires 4.5% annually, and the extra would fund more capital. Each percent is about $1 million in revenue [00:37:19].
* The slide's monthly bill impact assumes 3,000 gallons a month [00:37:56]. A commissioner cited a $1.32 increment at 3,000 gallons. Average use is about 5,000 gallons, and Byne said Steve Little would need to clarify the exact figure [00:49:11].
* Commissioner Wimberly said rates rose about 6% last year and "three something" before that, almost 14% over three years. He cited residents' complaints about meter reads and surprise bills [00:38:32].
* Byne's answer: the capital work must be paid for, and the city should avoid falling back into debt [00:39:39]. The AMI rollout adds a web portal and leak alerts [00:40:05]. Last year's increase was tied to canal damage; this one covers capital and across-the-board price increases. One 24-inch main budgeted at $2.3 million came in at $5.5 million [00:41:10].
* Asked whether grants could stabilize rates, Byne said that if plans work, "we should be able to reduce that rate substantially," and offered midyear or quarterly updates [00:42:03].
* Wimberly noted the roughly 50 open positions. Byne said a COLA would help retention, and the utility lost six operators in September [00:42:42].
* PFAS follow-up (questioner not identified) [00:44:31]: four or five of 21 wells had elevated PFAS. Those wells were taken offline and are pumped to contain the plume [00:45:11]. The 2024 EPA limit is 4 parts per trillion, and Augusta typically measures 10 to 12 [00:45:48].
* On succession, Byne said a strategic reorganization (for example, a chief operating officer role) is under consideration. The city has no formal succession program [00:48:06].
* Tap fees: sewer pre-tap fees rise from $125 to $325, commercial dwelling development fees go to $500 each, and fire line tap fees rise to recover equipment cost [00:50:19].
* Commissioner Pulliam asked how these compare with neighbors. Augusta's fees are lower and the city charges no impact fees, while neighbors charge up to $125,000 for an 8-inch tap [00:51:12]. Pulliam asked for Aiken, Columbia County, and North Augusta tap fees [00:52:07].

### Augusta Transit: budget overview (Administrator Allen)

* The 2027 recommendation is a little under 4% above the 2026 budget, driven mostly by depreciation, which is not new cash [00:59:03]. 2025 actuals exceeded both budget years [00:59:23].
* The operations contract (fixed route, paratransit, rural) is about 60% of the budget [00:59:37]. Allen called the contractor Transdev; Director Dottery later corrected it to MV Transportation [01:16:34]. The contract escalator is about 3%, depreciation rises with buses bought in 2025, staff costs are flat, and the indirect cost allocation falls [01:00:35].
* Revenue mix [01:01:21]:
  * Federal operating assistance covers about a third, mostly 50-50 reimbursement.
  * Local support (general fund, Urban Services Fund, transportation and tourism fee) covers about a third [01:01:47].
  * About $2.3 million a year in discretionary funds continues through 2032 [01:02:00].
  * Fares cover about 6% [01:02:07], and GDOT rural support a little over 1% [01:02:22].
* The general fund transfer cut in 2026 is being restored, and Urban Services Fund support is rising. This stabilizes current service only; it adds nothing. The aim is to end reliance on fund balance [01:02:34]. Allen said fare projections are still "a little optimistic" [01:03:24].
* Local share history: about half in 2020, under 10% in 2021 and 2022 (ARPA and federal grants), about a third since 2023 [01:03:40]. Fares have never covered more than about 5% of cost [01:04:59].
* Recap: no additional service cuts, substantially higher tax-funded support, and a need to revisit the service model [01:05:35].
* Wimberly asked whether restored funding brings back the routes and buses cut this year. Allen said no; it covers higher depreciation and other costs [01:08:26]. She said a new model needs to come before the commission [01:11:21].
* A commissioner asked about the tourism fee. It is about $1 per hotel-motel stay, shared among transit, the Laney Walker bonds, and the convention center [01:12:10].
* Commissioner Johnson (District 1) asked what a new model looks like. Allen cited demand shifting to South Augusta, the need for a route study, and smaller buses instead of large ones carrying six or seven riders [01:13:14].

### Augusta Transit: operations (Director Dottery)

* A route study (comprehensive operational analysis) scope is ready. Dottery aims to get it through procurement and to the commission before year end, with work starting in 2027 [01:17:03].
* The system has run nine routes for years, and fares have not changed since 2009 [01:17:43]. Only Routes 8 and 9 serve South Augusta, and rural ridership has fallen with urbanization [01:18:46].
* Microtransit is targeted for 2027. Dottery plans to combine FY2025 and FY2026 grants and meet with FTA in November [01:19:09]. Vehicle funding exists, but vehicles will not be bought until the program is ready [01:19:53].
* Fleet and service: 14 fixed-route buses (12 in daily use), 8 paratransit vans, 6 rural vans, 9 routes, and about 600 stops [01:21:47]. Last year's ridership: 412,080 fixed route, 17,436 ADA, 9,477 rural [01:22:06].
* Customer concerns:
  * One contractor employee cleans all shelters, so Dottery wants monthly contractor reports [01:22:57].
  * The bus wash is "a nightmare" [01:23:58].
  * Repairs at the new facility (pipes, AC) eat operating funds, and nonessential fixes are deferred [01:24:33].
  * Safety incidents sometimes require Richmond County deputies [01:25:36].
  * Reliability is the top priority [01:26:05].
* Peers: Columbus has 10 routes for about 202,000 people, Augusta 9 routes for 201,980, Macon 11, Savannah 18, and Athens 18 [01:26:52].
* Dottery said new technology is being installed to produce trustworthy ridership data [01:28:42].
* A FlixBus (Greyhound) contract brings in $167,000 over five years [01:29:30]. A shelter and bench program RFP is being prepared [01:30:16].
